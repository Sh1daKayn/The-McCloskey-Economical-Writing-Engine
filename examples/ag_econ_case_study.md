# Before & After: Agricultural Economics Example

The following example shows how a dull, abstract paragraph can become a sharper argument when it follows McCloskey's principles. It also demonstrates a fuller workflow: revision, diagnosis, and coaching.

## Example 1: Revision

### Before

> It is often the case that the agricultural supply chain may be significantly affected by a variety of exogenous factors that can lead to inefficiencies in the distribution of commodities, particularly in the context of market volatility and price transmission mechanisms.

### After

> When a drought shrinks the corn crop in Iowa, elevators bid more aggressively, truckers reroute loads, and feed mills scramble for soymeal. The network slows because each link has less buffer and every contract carries more risk.

## Why the After Version Works

| Feature | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Actor | Impersonal abstraction | Specific actors: elevators, truckers, feed mills |
| Voice | Passive and diffuse | Active and concrete |
| Evidence | Generalized claim | A vivid, plausible mechanism |
| Style | Inflated and vague | Lean and readable |

## Example 2: Style Diagnosis

### Diagnosis

The original passage sounds formal, but it hides the real mechanism. It uses general nouns and weak verbs instead of concrete agents and visible actions.

### Weaknesses

- Over-reliance on abstract nouns such as "factors," "inefficiencies," and "mechanisms"
- Passive structure that obscures agency
- Excessive cushioning that delays the main point

### Coaching Advice

- Replace abstract nouns with specific actors and events.
- Put the main claim in the first sentence.
- Practice writing one sentence that names the actor, the action, and the consequence.

## Example 3: Improvement Plan

A writer who produces this kind of prose should practice three exercises:

1. Rewrite each paragraph in one sentence that names the actor, the action, and the result.
2. Replace every nominalization with a verb.
3. Read the draft aloud and cut any sentence that does not earn its place.

This is the core of McCloskey's lesson: clear writing shows the mechanism. It names the actor, states the action, and makes the consequence visible.
